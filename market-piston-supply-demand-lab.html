What the motion illustrates

If demand pressure is greater than supply pressure, the piston moves right: quantity expands until the two pressures match. If supply pressure is greater, it moves left. On the graph, this is the movement toward the intersection of the downward-sloping demand curve and upward-sloping supply curve.

Historical context

Adam Smith described how self-interest, competition, and market prices coordinate economic activity. The familiar supply-and-demand curves were formalized by later economists. This laboratory connects that classical market tradition to a modern physical analogy; it is not a claim that markets literally obey gas laws.

Gas-to-market equations

All variables are normalized, so the model shows relationships rather than real units.

Demand strengthAD = ZD (nD / 50) TD
Supply cost pressureAS = ZS (nS / 50) TS
Demand curvePD(Q) = AD / Q
Supply curvePS(Q) = AS / (V - Q)
Equilibrium quantityQ* = V AD / (AD + AS)
Equilibrium priceP* = (AD + AS) / V

Label key

The analogy assigns a market meaning to each gas-model element.

PDDemand-side willingness-to-pay pressure
PSSupply-side marginal-cost pressure
QQuantity represented by demand-chamber volume
V-QRemaining supply-chamber volume
PD-PSExcess demand or excess supply force
P*Price where the market clears

Teaching model only. Real markets include expectations, institutions, income, inventories, market power, frictions, time delays, and many other forces. The gas chamber is a visual analogy for adjustment toward equilibrium, not a literal economic law or forecasting system.
